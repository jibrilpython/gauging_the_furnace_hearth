1. DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

Visual: "Kiln Record Bright" — light background, and the deliberate choice. This app sits in the same subject territory as the Pyrometer Forge but occupies a completely different operational position: where the Forge cataloged the instruments from the perspective of the furnace operator standing at the heat source, the Hearth approaches from the perspective of the pyrometry historian and the glassworks curator — the person who reads the Seger cone after firing, examines the Wedgwood cylinder after shrinkage, and holds the optical pyrometer up to the furnace mouth from a position of analytical distance. These are instruments assessed after the event, in the light of the laboratory and the kiln room, not in the darkness of the forge. The design language draws from the visual tradition of the ceramic technology publication, the glassworks quality record, and the pyrometry standards laboratory: warm white surfaces, precise ruled columns, and a palette pulled from the specific visual phenomena of high-temperature ceramic measurement — the deformed cone, the shrunken cylinder, the matched filament.

Mood: kiln-analytical, ceramic-precise, pyrometry-scholarly, glassworks-curatorial, thermal-historical.

Important: No dark forge aesthetics borrowed from the Pyrometer Forge — this is a fundamentally different relationship to the same heat. The Forge was dark because it stood at the furnace. The Hearth is light because it reads the evidence the furnace left behind: the bent cone, the shrunken clay, the calibrated color match. The analysis happens in daylight, and the UI honors that distinction.

3. TYPOGRAPHY

Display / Headings: Playfair Display (weight 700) — returns for the sixth time in the series, here in its most ceramically justified placement. The Seger cone system was published and distributed through exactly the Playfair typographic tradition — the Seger pyrometric cone catalog, the Wedgwood pyrometer instruction manual, and the ceramic technology publications of the German and British pottery industries all used the high-Victorian serif publishing tradition that Playfair references. At large sizes it carries the authority of a Josiah Wedgwood laboratory publication or a Royal Porcelain Works technical specification. Used for instrument names, cone series designations, and the app title.
Body / Descriptions: Inter (weight 300–400) — neutral and precise. For condition notes, glassworks history, deformation temperature notes, and all prose fields.
Monospace / Identifiers: IBM Plex Mono — for archive codes like GFH-SEGER-CONE-SK32-1886-DEU-BERLIN-PORCELAIN-WORKS-019, cone deformation temperature (T_def: 1,710°C), Wedgwood cylinder shrinkage (ΔL: 4.2mm), and all ceramic deformation simulator input and output parameters.

Important: Playfair Display at 24px and above only. Inter for all reading-size text. IBM Plex Mono for every deformation temperature, shrinkage measurement, and archive code — pyrometric cone specifications were calibrated against international temperature standards to within ±10°C, and the Wedgwood cylinder shrinkage was measured to fractions of a millimetre. The typography must carry that thermal precision.

CERAMIC DEFORMATION SIMULATOR — DESIGN SPECIFICATION

The ceramic deformation simulator is the defining technical feature of the app and the ninth simulation environment in the series. It models an eighth category of physical behavior — ceramic phase transformation and viscous deformation under sustained high temperature — the first simulation in the series to model the behavior of a non-metallic, non-optical, non-biological material system under extreme thermal conditions.

Input environment: light panel (
#F0EAE0) within the overall kiln record ivory — following the Anvil Horn and the Cable Strands in placing the simulation in a light environment. The ceramic deformation simulator models a process assessed in the light of the kiln room after firing, not in the darkness of the furnace interior. The warm ivory of the simulation panel is slightly darker and more amber than the archival background, referencing the specific quality of light in a ceramics laboratory where fired specimens are examined under directional inspection lighting.
Input fields in IBM Plex Mono: cone composition (from material database: Seger standard, Orton standard, custom clay body), cone geometry (height mm, base width mm, thickness mm), heating rate (°C/hour), peak temperature (°C), soak time at peak (minutes), kiln atmosphere (oxidising, reducing, neutral), and — for Wedgwood cylinders — initial cylinder length (mm) and clay body composition (standard Wedgwood, custom).
Cone deformation diagram: SVG rendered in real time — a sequence of cone silhouettes at 50°C temperature intervals from 50°C below the rated deformation temperature to 50°C above it, showing the progressive bending of the cone tip from upright through 45° bend to full slump. Each silhouette rendered in the thermal color appropriate to its temperature — deep red at the lowest modeled temperature transitioning through orange to cone deformation orange at the rated deformation point and into bright amber above it. The current modeled state highlighted with a small temperature label in IBM Plex Mono. This is the most visually continuous motif in any simulation in the series — the cone deformation is a smooth, temperature-dependent process, and the SVG sequence should render it as such.
Viscous flow model: a secondary output showing the computed viscosity of the cone body at the modeled temperature, plotted as a curve on a log-viscosity vs temperature graph. The deformation point is marked where viscosity crosses the critical threshold for gravitational slump — the moment the cone tip begins to bend. Rendered in cone deformation orange at the threshold, pyrometer blue for the certified calibration points from the Seger or Orton standards.
Deformation outputs in IBM Plex Mono: predicted deformation temperature (T_def: 1,710°C), predicted bend angle at peak temperature (θ: 67°), Wedgwood shrinkage prediction (ΔL: 4.2mm), heating rate sensitivity (ΔT per 100°C/hr: +8°C), atmosphere correction (Δatm: −15°C in reducing), and pyrometric equivalent temperature (PET: SK32 equivalent).
Optical pyrometer matching panel: a tertiary section unique to this simulator — for disappearing-filament pyrometer records, the panel displays the computed filament current at the modeled temperature alongside the historical calibration table from the instrument's original certificate, allowing a historian to verify that a historical optical pyrometer reading is consistent with the simultaneously recorded Seger cone deformation state. This closes the loop between the two primary pyrometric measurement systems of the 19th century — the ceramic cone and the optical pyrometer — and allows cross-validation of historical temperature records that used both systems simultaneously.

Important: The ceramic deformation simulator is the most materially exotic in the series — it models the thermomechanical behavior of a fired clay body, a material system that responds to temperature through viscous flow rather than elastic deformation, phase transformation rather than mechanical failure. The three-state cone sequence on each list card is the most thermally narrative motif in the series — it tells a complete measurement story in three small silhouettes, and a pyrometry historian should read the measurement event from the card before reading any temperature value. Cone deformation orange at the deformation point means the instrument performed its measurement function completely and the temperature record is valid. Pyrometer blue in the optical matching panel means the disappearing-filament reading is consistent with the cone evidence. Critical errors red means the historical temperature record contains an inconsistency that requires investigation. That distinction carries the weight of a century of industrial quality control — in the glassworks and the porcelain kiln, a temperature record that cannot be validated against two independent pyrometric systems is a record that cannot be trusted.
